# Damage is measured in tissue condition

A blow's damage is measured in condition of reference tissue: 1.0 is enough to destroy one whole layer of toughness 1 in a body of the reference man's weight, and a tissue's toughness is how many units it takes to destroy it. This was the original intent, which is why the old weapons had values above 1. There is no conversion factor at the body boundary. Weapon and animal blows are set on this unit: a spear thrust (2) gets through a person's or a wolf's flesh to an organ, and a wolf's bite (0.75) does not. Toughness grows with the cube root of body weight. Blood is 70 ml a kilo, and bleeding grows with weight to the three-quarters (Kleiber), so a big animal bleeds out more slowly. A wound bleeds from what it destroyed, and deeper tissue bleeds more. Clothing protects only the regions it covers.

The kind of blow sets how much of a tissue it meets. That share is the tissue's effective toughness: what it can stop of one blow, and never more than it has left. The tissue still loses one condition per unit of its own toughness, so a narrow blow cannot destroy a whole layer at once, only over several blows. We chose this over effective toughness setting both what a layer stops and what it loses. Under that rule, passing a layer meant destroying it. Each region has one skin layer, so one wolf bite destroyed a region, and every blow killed, in probes, a third to a half of bodies outright and all of them within a day.

The first framing (C2) said pierce and sharp face less soft tissue than blunt does. The blunt decision (Q43) said soft tissue gives little to a club, which carries its force to bone. These conflict on blunt against sharp. Blunt follows Q43: it faces the least soft tissue and the most bone, so a club breaks bone that an edge spends itself before reaching. Pierce still goes deepest.

A club bruises skin and muscle without opening them, so only the bone and organs it breaks bleed; a cut bleeds however small. What a tissue shrugs off before it is hurt does not grow with size. Healing is on the same unit, so a big body mends more slowly, and organs, being small, mend fast.

Joules of kinetic energy and tissue thickness in centimetres were considered and deferred; maybe later.
